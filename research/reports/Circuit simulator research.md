# Make voltage visible, keep every answer exact

Homebase's circuit simulator should be a small, exact, explainable bench, not a SPICE clone. It should have a modified nodal analysis (MNA) engine written in vanilla JavaScript, a 2D SVG schematic editor, and a 3D "potential landscape" where node voltage is height. It should hide results until Connor commits a prediction, and show the equations behind every answer. No mainstream tool combines live, animated intuition with SPICE-grade analysis and step-by-step explanation. Falstad animates but has no Bode analysis, LTspice is accurate but opaque, and Multisim Live shut down on September 15, 2026. That gap is the reason to build one. The research also says what *not* to build. The best evidence supports **predict-before-reveal** (it roughly doubles learning gains from demonstrations) and **potential-centred visuals**. Animated current dots, the feature every simulator shows off, had **no measurable effect** on exam scores in the one controlled test. Homebase already has a tested Python MNA solver and a schematic drawing style, and the JS engine can be checked against both. Circuit Foundry gives Connor a design rulebook that has been tested in use: visuals come only from solver state, and graphics clocks never advance the simulation. Its React/Babylon code does not port to Homebase's no-build pages. The simulator stays useful through the degree if it reads and writes SPICE netlists, because OSU's electronics courses hand out SPICE models. Its lasting advantage is explanation, which LTspice will never offer. Phase 1 should cover ECE 201 weeks 1–10 in full: DC with exact fractions, dependent sources, op-amps, switched first-order transients, the 3D height view for DC, and a "check my hand solution" mode. AC, nonlinear devices and SPICE import come in later phases that match the course sequence.

## Nobody combines live animation, SPICE-grade analysis and explanation

The tools students use split into two camps that rarely meet. **Real-time simulators** (Falstad CircuitJS1, EveryCircuit, iCircuit, PhET, SimulIDE, Tinkercad) run a continuous transient simulation and animate it. **Batch SPICE tools** (LTspice, ngspice and its front-ends KiCad and Qucs-S, CircuitLab, Multisim) offer the full analysis set of operating point, DC sweep, transient, AC, noise, Fourier and parameter stepping, but show results in plot windows away from the schematic. Falstad's documentation describes only transient simulation plus a scope FFT, so a student cannot draw a filter's Bode plot without sweeping a source by hand ([Falstad directions](https://www.falstad.com/circuit/directions.html); [pfalstad/circuitjs1](https://github.com/pfalstad/circuitjs1)). Falstad shipped a TypeScript rewrite as version 5.0 on October 18, 2026, and no AC analysis was seen in its commit log. Christie and Trinidad named the cost of the SPICE side back in 1998: results shown "away from the circuit" keep the relationship abstract, and PSpice in a first course "may teach more about PSpice than about the circuits" ([Christie & Trinidad, ASEE 1998](https://peer.asee.org/visualizing-pedagogical-circuits.pdf)).

The complaints are consistent, and they explain what Connor's tool should be. Falstad is praised for speed and intuition. UIUC's ECE 110 starts students on it because it "yields results in seconds", then moves them to LTspice for "better precision, better visualization tools" ([UIUC ECE110 handout](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/ECE110/content/labs/Experiments/Skill_Sim_Falstad.pdf)). Users report that Falstad is "not accurate like LTspice" and that two coils do not form a transformer because it has no magnetic coupling ([EEVblog](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/beginners/simulation-of-a-blinking-circuit-(falstad)/msg3412558/)). Its source code shows shortcuts that are fine for intuition and wrong for homework. It models capacitors as **1e8 Ω resistors at DC**, ties floating nodes to ground through 1e8 Ω, and gives capacitors a 1 mV default initial voltage so oscillators start ([CirSim.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CirSim.java); [CapacitorElm.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CapacitorElm.java)). Tinkercad let a motor circuit work without the flyback diode it would need in real life ([Norwegian Creations](https://www.norwegiancreations.com/2018/11/a-first-impression-of-tinkercad-circuits/)). Hosted tools keep disappearing: Multisim Live is gone, and PartSim "simply gets stuck at app loading screen" ([AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/software/partsim/about/)). A local, file-based tool inside Homebase avoids that risk entirely.

The 3D idea has academic history but no maintained product. Doering drew node voltages "as the node height above the reference plane", with current as stripe width. CktViz (1998) showed current and voltage directly on the circuit and found "a positive and significant effect" on intro students' qualitative understanding in a small assessment ([Christie & Trinidad 1998](https://peer.asee.org/visualizing-pedagogical-circuits.pdf)). Dartmouth's MATLAB "3-D Circuits" toolbox was last updated in 2002 ([Dartmouth 3-D Circuits](https://3dcircuits.engineering.dartmouth.edu/)). No current, public voltage-as-height simulator turned up, so this would be new ground.

Licensing settles what can be reused. CircuitJS1, KiCad, Qucs-S and SimulIDE are copyleft (GPL/AGPL). They can be read for algorithms but not copied into Homebase. EEcircuit, spice-ts and repath are MIT and could be embedded ([pfalstad/circuitjs1](https://github.com/pfalstad/circuitjs1); [spice-ts](https://github.com/spicets/spice-ts); [repath](https://github.com/Agustin-Delgado/repath)). ngspice itself is "Modified BSD" with LGPL islands (KLU, numparam) and one GPL piece (the XSPICE table model) ([ngspice COPYING](https://sourceforge.net/p/ngspice/ngspice/ci/master/tree/COPYING?format=raw)). MNA, companion models and Newton-Raphson are textbook methods that anyone can implement.

## Prediction and potential, not dots, drive measured learning gains

### The misconceptions to target are about current and potential

Thirty years of research converge on one cluster of errors. Students treat current as the primary quantity that gets "used up". They picture the battery as a **constant-current source**, the idea students used most in DIRECT interviews. They reason sequentially, as if a part affects only what comes "after" it. And they have no working idea of potential as a property of each point in the circuit ([Engelhardt & Beichner 2004](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0304040); [Bodensiek et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02522)). A systematic review of 15 first-year circuit-analysis studies found **20 misconceptions in 8 categories**. Blind use of Ohm's law was cited by 7 studies and sequential reasoning by 6. Notably, **none of the 15 studies tried to remediate them** ([Sambamurthy & Edgcomb 2018](https://zybooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Paper__Systematic_literature_review_of_misconceptions_in_linear_circuit_analysis__1_.pdf)). AC difficulties are documented but less studied. They are phase, lead and lag, instantaneous versus RMS values, and what is actually "alternating". A Swinburne phasor pre-test averaged **38%** (N=86) ([UTS OPUS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/153842)), and Kautz found Kirchhoff and phase difficulties persisted whatever the student population or instructor ([SEFI summary](https://sefi.be/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/081-sum.pdf)). No research turned up on passive-sign-convention or ground-node errors specifically. Those are ECE 201 staples, so the simulator should handle them by design rather than by evidence.

The review also warns that textbooks themselves carry misconceptions such as "voltage source provides charge", and that the water-pipe metaphor produces "current pools behind resistors" ([Sambamurthy & Edgcomb 2018](https://zybooks.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Paper__Systematic_literature_review_of_misconceptions_in_linear_circuit_analysis__1_.pdf)). Analogies are generative: learners import the source domain's structure, wrong inferences included ([Gentner & Gentner 1983](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerGentner83.pdf)). The simulator's own animations and help text therefore need the same scrutiny as its maths.

### Potential as height has the strongest visual evidence

Potential-centred representations have the best support. In a quasi-experimental study of **790 students**, a curriculum that treats voltage as an "electric pressure difference" outperformed traditional teaching with **d = 0.94** ([Burde & Wilhelm 2020](https://journals.aps.org/prper/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevPhysEducRes.16.020153)). Bodensiek et al. used 3D-printed height models in which 1 cm equals 1 V. Correct voltage definitions rose from **24% to 70%**. The share of students who wrongly gave the source voltage between every pair of points fell from **75% to 5%**. Correct answers for both voltages across two series bulbs rose from 29% to 63% ([Bodensiek et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.02522)). The same study gives two cautions the design must respect. There was **no significant gain for parallel or mixed circuits**. And height plus moving electrons creates a sign problem: negative electrons would have to travel *uphill*, so the authors redefined the charge as positive. The study had 59 eighth-graders, no control group and physical models rather than software, so treat it as strong direction, not proof for ECE 201.

What the evidence implies for "truthful 3D" is precise. Every wire segment on one node sits at a single height, forming an equipotential plateau. Height changes only across elements. Resistors appear as ramps, and ideal sources as vertical lifts. Moving the ground node lifts or drops the whole landscape rigidly, which teaches that only differences matter. A loop that returns to its starting height makes KVL something you can see. Bodensiek's null result for parallel circuits means the height view alone will not teach current division. It needs companion tasks that compare branch currents, with a KCL readout at each junction.

### Prediction before reveal doubles gains; light guidance beats recipes

The best-quantified interaction finding comes from Crouch et al.'s demonstration study. Watching a demo raised correct explanations from 22% to only 24%, which was not significant. **Predicting first** raised them to 30%, "nearly quadrupling" the normalized gain, for about two extra minutes per demo ([Crouch et al. 2004](https://www.otffeo.on.ca/en/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2014/11/Mazur_demo-article.pdf)). One detail matters for design. Students choosing from a list of outcomes were right 26% of the time, against 41% for open-ended predictions, so the prompts should ask for numbers or rankings, not multiple choice. PhET's interview research found "minimal but nonzero guidance" gives the most productive exploration ([Adams et al. 2008](https://vivo.colorado.edu/display/pubid_118958)). In a related PhET study, heavy guidance cut use of unprompted features from **85% to 9%** ([Chamberlain et al. 2014](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2014/rp/c4rp00009a)). The simulator's exercises should therefore state goals ("make R3's current 2 mA without touching the source"), not click-by-click recipes.

Simulation can also beat the real bench. In a large intro course, students who did the DC lab with PhET's kit outperformed students using real bulbs. That held on conceptual questions and on assembling a *real* circuit ([Finkelstein et al. 2005](https://journals.aps.org/prper/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevSTPER.1.010103)). In lecture, a simulated demo gave a **47% larger relative gain** than a real one, with one question set rising from 59.8% to 92.1% correct against 61.2% to 83.1% for the real demo ([Keller et al. 2006](https://data.issuelab.org/resources/1363/1363.pdf)). These are intro-physics results with bulbs and qualitative questions. Whether they carry over to quantitative nodal, mesh and Thévenin work has not been established.

### Animation earns its place only when it tells the truth

The same Keller study ran the experiment that matters for current dots. A group with electron dots (N=65) and a group without them (N=90) scored the same on exam questions: **75.0% vs 73.7%, p = 0.8**. The no-dots group even rated the lab more positively ([Keller et al. 2006](https://data.issuelab.org/resources/1363/1363.pdf)). Tversky's review reaches the same conclusion more broadly. Animation shows no clear advantage over well-designed still images, and "judicious use of interactivity" is the proposed remedy ([Tversky et al. 2002](https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs448b/papers/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)). Dots are cheap and students like them, so keep them as a secondary layer under strict rules. They show conventional current by default, with an explicit electron-flow toggle. Spacing is uniform and conserved, so markers never bunch behind a resistor. Speed is labelled as a *visual encoding* of magnitude, not drift velocity. And the dots stop entirely under `prefers-reduced-motion`. Falstad's split between simulation speed and dot speed is the precedent for treating animation speed as a display choice ([Falstad directions](https://www.falstad.com/circuit/directions.html)).

Two other visual choices follow from the evidence. Ainsworth's DeFT framework says multiple representations help only when the learner is supported in translating between them ([Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/23577631/deft-a-conceptual-framework-for-considering-learning-with-multiple-representations)). So selecting a node should highlight it in the schematic, the 3D landscape, the node table and the scope at once. Falstad's green/red voltage scheme is a common colour-vision-deficiency pair, so Homebase should use a two-colour scale from its own theme tokens and always pair colour with numbers. Power should be a *signed* quantity, absorbed or delivered, which turns Falstad's hover readout, where "negative power means delivering" ([UIUC ECE110 handout](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/ECE110/content/labs/Experiments/Skill_Sim_Falstad.pdf)), into a direct drill on the passive sign convention. PhET's written list of idealizations (ideal meters, wire resistivity of at least 1e-10 Ω·m, minimum battery resistance) is the model for an always-visible assumptions panel ([PhET CCK model.md](https://github.com/phetsims/circuit-construction-kit-common/blob/main/doc/model.md)).

## A small, exact MNA engine beats embedding SPICE for ECE 201

### One formulation, with student-readable errors

Modified nodal analysis is the right core. It adds one current unknown and one constraint row for each element whose current cannot be written from node voltages: voltage sources, voltage-controlled and current-controlled voltage sources (VCVS, CCVS), ideal op-amp outputs and 0 V ammeters ([Ho, Ruehli & Brennan 1975](https://research.ibm.com/publications/the-modified-nodal-approach-to-network-analysis); [Bürmen, U. Ljubljana](https://zrojec.fe.uni-lj.si/cao/modified-nodal-analysis/)). The ideal op-amp is a nullor. Its output current becomes an unknown, its constraint row is v₊ − v₋ = 0, and it is valid only with negative feedback. The engine must detect a missing feedback path and say so rather than return garbage. Ideal closed switches and wires should be handled by **merging nodes**, as CircuitJS does, not by tiny resistances. That avoids ill-conditioned matrices, and a Falstad fork found that tiny-resistor wires broke its scope display ([CirSim.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CirSim.java); [hausen/circuit-simulator #1](https://github.com/hausen/circuit-simulator/issues/1)).

Errors are where a student tool wins or loses. CircuitJS checks the circuit's structure before solving and reports "Voltage source/wire loop with no resistance!" and "Capacitor loop with no resistance!", marking bad connections with red dots. spice-ts emits typed `FLOATING_NODE`, `NO_DC_PATH_TO_GROUND` and `VOLTAGE_SOURCE_LOOP` diagnostics instead of "singular matrix" ([CirSim.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CirSim.java); [spice-ts](https://github.com/spicets/spice-ts)). The checks are cheap graph algorithms. Union-find over zero-impedance edges finds voltage-source loops. A connectivity check without the current-source edges finds current-source cutsets. A breadth-first search from ground finds floating subcircuits. Each finding should highlight the offending parts. One rule should be firm: **never silently add a leak conductance** the way CircuitJS's 1e8 Ω tie does. A homework checker that quietly patches a floating node produces an answer that looks plausible and is wrong.

### Dense LU, exact fractions, and equations straight from the stamps

Student circuits have well under 100 unknowns. A dense LU factorization costs about (2/3)n³ operations, roughly 0.7 million at n = 100, which takes well under a millisecond in JavaScript even on a phone. Sparse solvers matter only at thousands of nodes. ngspice keeps KLU as an *optional* solver for large circuits ([ngspice manual](https://ngspice.sourceforge.io/docs/ngspice-html-manual/manual.xhtml)), and CircuitJS uses its own dense LU ([CirSim.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CirSim.java)). About 150 lines of LU code with partial pivoting over `Float64Array` beats any library here. Pivoting is mandatory because voltage-source rows put zeros on the diagonal. A pivot-ratio check should flag near-singular systems, mirroring the condition-number guard (reject above 1e12) in Homebase's Python solver ([circuits_dc.py](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/generators/circuits_dc.py)).

The feature LTspice cannot match is **exact rational arithmetic**. With integer or simple decimal component values, Gaussian elimination over BigInt fractions gives exact node voltages, so the tool can show **12/7 V** beside 1.714 V. That is how hand-analysis answers look. The engine falls back to floating point when values are irrational or numerators grow too large. Each stamp also knows which element created it, so the engine can print its own equations in readable form ("Node 2: (v₂ − v₁)/R₁ + v₂/R₂ = 0") and the matrix form, which covers a "show me the nodal equations" feature with no Python. Lcapy can produce symbolic nodal, mesh and MNA equations and Thévenin equivalents for linear circuits ([Lcapy docs](https://lcapy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/overview.html)), but its license was not confirmed. SymPy 1.14 is already in Homebase's requirements, so symbolic, letters-not-numbers work can wait for a later server endpoint.

### Transients: companion models, and get t = 0⁻ and t = 0⁺ right

Capacitors and inductors become Norton companion models, a conductance plus a history current source, re-stamped each step. Backward Euler is robust. Trapezoidal integration is more accurate for RLC circuits and filters but rings after sudden switching ([CircuitJS1 INTERNALS.md](https://github.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/blob/master/INTERNALS.md)). Production SPICE handles this by resetting to a first-order step at t = 0 and at every breakpoint (a source edge or a switch event), and by bounding local truncation error with variable step sizes ([U. Ljubljana lecture 9](https://fides.fe.uni-lj.si/~zigar/CAO/_lectures/_09_lecture_more.php)). CircuitJS has **no truncation-error control**. It halves the step only when Newton iteration fails, which is fine for animation and weak for answers ([CirSim.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CirSim.java)). For ECE 201's first-order circuits, a fixed small step with trapezoidal integration, a backward-Euler step at each breakpoint and a user-visible maximum step is more trustworthy than aggressive adaptive stepping. Error-controlled variable steps and a Gear (BDF2) option can come with second-order circuits.

Initial conditions matter more for learning than for numerics. The engine should compute the operating point with the *pre-switch* topology, with capacitors open and inductors shorted, then start the transient with the *post-switch* topology. That mirrors exactly how textbooks pose t = 0⁻ and t = 0⁺ problems. A "use initial conditions" option skips the operating point for problems that state v_C(0) directly. Capacitors default to zero volts, not Falstad's 1 mV.

### AC and nonlinear devices reuse the same engine later

AC analysis is the same MNA system in complex numbers, with C becoming jωC and L becoming 1/(jωL). A frequency sweep is one complex LU solve per frequency point, and SPICE has combined DC, small-signal AC and transient analysis in one nodal program since 1973 ([Nagel & Pederson 1973](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/1973/ERL-m-382.pdf)). Nonlinear devices use Newton-Raphson, re-stamping each device's linearized model every iteration. ngspice's convergence test is |Δv| ≤ RELTOL·v_max + VNTOL, with RELTOL 1e-3 and VNTOL 1 µV ([ngspice manual §1.4](https://ngspice.sourceforge.io/docs/ngspice-html-manual/manual.xhtml)). When plain Newton fails, the engine falls back first to gmin stepping and then to source stepping ([LTspice options via U. Ljubljana](https://lpvo.fe.uni-lj.si/wp-content/uploads/izobrazevanje/mag/nev/LTSpice/Simulator_options.txt)). One choice matters for teaching. Textbook diode problems use the constant-voltage-drop model, so an **ideal or constant-drop diode** solved by checking each diode's on/off state should come before the Shockley exponential, because it reproduces the answers Connor's homework expects. Op-amp rail saturation is the same kind of piecewise problem.

### Verification is three layers, and Homebase already holds the first oracle

Homebase's rule that keys come "only from real published solutions or from answers computed and checked with code" ([Homebase README](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/README.md)) means the engine must earn trust before it can grade anything. The first layer is analytic tests per stamp and topology: dividers, bridges, superposition, Thévenin, op-amp configurations, and RC and RL closed forms. These require exact equality in rational mode, and integrators must show O(h) error for backward Euler and O(h²) for trapezoidal on an RC step. The second layer is **differential testing against Homebase's own Python `mna()`**. It already handles R, V, I, VCCS, VCVS and ideal op-amps, and it asserts KCL at every node and power balance ([circuits_dc.py](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/generators/circuits_dc.py)). Later, ngspice runs on the same netlists with SPICE's own tolerances ([eecircuit-engine README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/eecircuit-engine@1.7.0/README.md)). The third layer is golden problems with official keys from Homebase's 344 ECE 201 problems. Error paths need tests too. Every malformed circuit (floating node, voltage loop, op-amp without feedback) must produce its specific message, never NaN. spice-ts's 100-circuit ngspice parity benchmark and Falstad's trace-diff tooling for its C++ port are precedents for this discipline ([spice-ts](https://github.com/spicets/spice-ts); [CircuitJS1 cxx/README](https://github.com/pfalstad/circuitjs1/tree/master/cxx)).

ngspice compiled to WebAssembly is the obvious engine to add later, not the foundation. `eecircuit-engine` takes an ngspice netlist through `setNetList()` and `runSim()` entirely in the browser ([eecircuit-engine README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/eecircuit-engine@1.7.0/README.md)). But it works in batches, its file size, start-up time and exact license were not found, and it cannot explain its answers. It belongs in phase 3 as a lazily loaded "verify with SPICE" button and as the engine for transistor work.

## Edit in SVG, save as versioned JSON, render 3D from the same truth

### Editing borrows KiCad's rules and EveryCircuit's touch model

Desktop editors agree on a small set of patterns. **W** draws a wire, **R** rotates, **X/Y** mirror and **E** edits properties. A coarse snap grid also defines connectivity. Junction dots are added automatically only where a wire *ends* on another wire, never at a plain crossing ([KiCad Eeschema manual](https://docs.kicad.org/9.0/en/eeschema/eeschema.html)). KiCad separates Move, which breaks connections, from Drag, which rubber-bands the wires. Beginners almost always want Drag, so it should be the default, with a modifier key to detach. KiCad's manual warns that off-grid parts and near-miss endpoints are the recurring connectivity failure. The answer is a coarse grid plus a visible marker on every unconnected endpoint. On phones, EveryCircuit avoids precise dragging altogether: tap a part to add it, tap one terminal and then another to wire them, and let automatic routing draw the wire ([EveryCircuit demo](https://everycircuit.com/circuit/4947946720985088/everycircuit-demo-1); [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id797157761)). Homebase's UI plan already requires 36 px tap targets and no sideways scrolling ([UI-PLAN.md](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/design/UI-PLAN.md)), and tap-tap wiring with large invisible hit areas around pins meets that. Falstad's model of a part as a segment between two snapped endpoints is simple to hit-test and save ([CircuitElm.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CircuitElm.java)). It suits resistors, sources, capacitors and inductors, while op-amps and dependent sources need explicit pins.

Value entry needs care because of one SPICE trap: suffixes are case-insensitive, so "M" means *milli* and "MEG" means mega. The parser should accept "4.7k", "2.2u" and "2.2µ", show how it read the value ("1 MΩ?"), and refuse ambiguous input rather than guess. The 2D schematic should be **SVG, not canvas**. That lets every element be a focusable DOM node with an ARIA label, and research found novices struggle with plain alt-text circuit descriptions ([Dartmouth AccessibleCircuit](https://cs.dartmouth.edu/%7Ezheer/files/AccessibleCircuit.pdf)). A node-by-node list ("Node Vout: R1 pin 2, C1 pin 1") is a screen-reader view and Connor's own debugging table at the same time.

### Own the format; treat SPICE and Falstad as doors

The canonical file should be Homebase's own versioned JSON with **explicit nets as well as layout**. It should store both the typed string ("1k") and the parsed number, and carry an integer version with a migration step for each version. Explicit nets mean a netlist imported without a drawing is still a valid document. Falstad's text format infers nodes only from coincident endpoints, so converting it is "mostly a coordinate problem" ([All About Circuits](https://forum.allaboutcircuits.com/threads/conversion-of-netlist-to-schematic.206055/post-1975377)). SPICE netlist export is cheap and valuable from day one, because it lets Connor paste any circuit into LTspice for a second opinion. One known pitfall: the F and H sources (current-controlled current and voltage sources) need a named 0 V source in the controlling branch, which the exporter must insert. Falstad's one-line-per-element format (`type x1 y1 x2 y2 flags params`) is easy to round-trip ([CircuitElm.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sharpie7/circuitjs1/master/src/com/lushprojects/circuitjs1/client/CircuitElm.java)). Importing it is a later convenience, and Falstad's example circuits are GPL content, so textbook circuits should be rebuilt from scratch. KiCad's `.kicad_sch` format requires embedding full symbol definitions ([KiCad file format](https://dev-docs.kicad.org/en/file-formats/sexpr-schematic/)) and is not worth the effort for a learner.

Auto-layout deserves scepticism. netlistsvg with ELK lays out analog parts from a netlist, but the results look like dataflow diagrams rather than textbook schematics ([netlistsvg](https://github.com/nturley/netlistsvg)). Circuit Foundry's audit found the same thing first-hand. Its ELK layout of a 20-part CPU left "labels collide... cables cross everywhere", and the "after" screenshot still looked cramped ([CF audit prompt](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Circuit%20Foundry%20audit%202026-10-08/Codex%20prompt.md)). Textbook circuits read best from authored layouts. Homebase's generators already draw ladders and parts with known positions, so they can emit layouts directly. Image-to-netlist recognition is real research, but it is not phase-1 material. SINA reports **96.47% component-detection F1** ([SINA, arXiv 2601.22114](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.22114)), yet a hand-drawn pipeline reached only 53.37% on orientation ([arXiv 2402.11093](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11093)), and connectivity remains the weak point. The realistic later design is an AI draft placed next to the source image, which Connor corrects.

### Three.js, lazily loaded, drawing a potential landscape

For a modest scene that must load on a phone, **Three.js is about 168 kB** (min+gzip) against **about 1.4 MB for Babylon.js** ([Utsubo comparison](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-vs-babylonjs-vs-playcanvas-comparison)). Babylon's cascaded shadows have been blamed for mobile slowness ([Babylon forum](https://forum.babylonjs.com/t/babylonjs-takes-longer-to-render-the-first-frame-on-mobile-compared-to-threejs/41213)). Circuit Foundry used Babylon with WebGPU, SSAO and glow pipelines and still missed its 120 fps goal, reaching only **26–28 fps on a Radeon 890M** in Cinematic mode ([What is new in 0.4](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Codex/2026-10-06/are/outputs/CircuitFoundry/docs/What%20is%20new%20in%200.4.txt)). The lesson is restraint: Lambert or basic materials, no real-time shadows, rendering only on demand when nothing animates, and a pause when the tab is hidden. A circuit needs perhaps a few thousand current markers. A single `InstancedMesh` handles that easily, as long as its `count` is set explicitly ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/how-to-draw-spheres-more-than-1000000/19731)). A marker's position along its wire can be computed in the shader from time and branch current.

The 3D scene should be **generated from the same JSON and the same solver snapshot as the 2D view**, never edited separately. Each net's wires sit at z = V(node) relative to the chosen ground. Parts become ramps or lifts between plateaus, and resistors can glow by the power they absorb. During transient playback the scene must show its time base, for example "1 ms of circuit time = 1 s on screen". Between solver steps it shows the stored values or a labelled interpolation, never invented physics.

## OSU's degree forks after ECE 203, so interoperability decides longevity

OSU now offers one Electrical and Computer Engineering BS with a 4-credit sophomore core: ECE 200, **201 DC and Transient Circuits**, 202 AC and Frequency Dependent Circuits, 203 Continuous-Time Signal Processing and 204 Digital Logic. In the junior year students choose **four of six** Foundations courses (310, 320, 330, 350, 370, 380) ([OSU Catalog, ECE BS](https://catalog.oregonstate.edu/college-departments/engineering/school-electrical-engineering-computer-science/electrical-computer-engineering-bs-hbs/)). No public syllabus confirmed which simulator ECE 201's lab or ECE 202 uses, so Connor should check the Canvas page when the term opens.

## Homebase supplies the solver oracle and drawing style; Circuit Foundry supplies the rules

Homebase sets firm constraints. It is FastAPI with Jinja templates and small vanilla JS files, with **no bundler and no npm**. Static files get a cache-busting version through `static_v()`. Colours are CSS tokens that swap under `[data-theme="dark"]`, and theme changes broadcast a `homebase-theme` event ([pages.py](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/routes/pages.py); [app.js](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/static/app.js); [style.css](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/static/style.css)). Interactive note widgets run in sandboxed iframes with no API access and a 900 px height cap ([widgets.py](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/widgets.py)). That suits small illustrations but rules out the full simulator, which must be its own page with its own route, template and static scripts. Plain `<script type="module">` files with relative imports work without a bundler, so the simulator can be split into many small modules (solver, editor, views) while staying within the no-build rule. The phone reaches Homebase over plain HTTP on Tailscale, which is not a secure context. Service workers and clipboard writing are unavailable there, and KaTeX loads from a CDN ([base.html](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/templates/base.html)), so equation display needs a connection. Every action in Homebase is a function in `services.py`, called by the pages, the API and the MCP server alike ([Homebase README](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/README.md)). Saved circuits should follow the same path, so they appear in the activity log and Claude can read them.

The biggest asset is already in the repo. `circuits_dc.py` (1,860 lines) contains a tested MNA solver over an `El` netlist (R, V, I, VCCS, VCVS, ideal op-amp) with KCL and power-balance self-checks. It also has `Part` and `Ladder` SVG drawers that define Homebase's schematic look: ink strokes, surface-filled bodies and terracotta arrows. `circuits_transient.py` checks RC and RL answers by integrating the circuit's differential equation with RK4 ([circuits_dc.py](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/generators/circuits_dc.py); [circuits_transient.py](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/app/generators/circuits_transient.py)). This gives the JS engine a built-in second method for checking its keys. It also enables the most valuable integration: an **"Open in simulator"** button on each of the 65 generated problems, converting the generator's own parts and layout into simulator JSON. Homebase has no JavaScript test runner ([PLAN.md](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Homebase/PLAN.md)). The practical bridge is a shared set of JSON test vectors written by pytest from the Python solver. A browser self-test page replays them, and Node replays them too if installed. Circuit Foundry's Vite toolchain suggests Node is already on the laptop.

Circuit Foundry is a design reference, not a code source. It is a React 19, TypeScript, Babylon and elkjs Electron app for *digital* logic, with no concept of current or MNA ([CF package.json](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Codex/2026-10-06/are/outputs/CircuitFoundry/package.json)). Its renderer is tied to React and nand2tetris types. Its principles transfer intact: "Graphics clocks never advance the simulation", "Spatial layout never changes logical topology", every glow and number comes from real simulator state, and "No animation may imply physical timing that is not modeled" ([CF AGENTS.md](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Codex/2026-10-06/are/outputs/CircuitFoundry/AGENTS.md); [premium UI prompt](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Circuit%20Foundry%20audit%202026-10-08/Codex%20prompt%20-%20premium%20UI.md)). So does its architecture of a solver that emits immutable snapshots, with views written as pure functions of graph, layout and snapshot ([types.ts](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Codex/2026-10-06/are/outputs/CircuitFoundry/src/shared/types.ts)). Its failures are just as instructive. App.tsx grew to **5,408 lines** despite a refactor goal ([What is new in 0.4](file:///C:/Users/conno/Documents/Codex/2026-10-06/are/outputs/CircuitFoundry/docs/What%20is%20new%20in%200.4.txt)). Glow-only cues misled: cyan selection read as a logic 1. Generic auto-layout stayed cramped. The new simulator should start with small files, use outlines and labels rather than glow alone for meaning, and use authored layouts.

## Conclusion

The research turns the question around. The hard part is not a simulator that computes correctly, since Homebase already has one in Python. It is a tool that refuses to let Connor skip the thinking. The strongest evidence behind the design is predict-then-reveal and potential-centred visuals. The most-imitated feature, animated current, had no measured effect on learning. So the 3D view is justified as a picture of potential, and the dots are justified only because they are honest and cheap. Exact fractions, located error messages and equations printed from the stamps are what separate a study tool from LTspice. They also make the simulator's answers checkable under Homebase's existing rule for keys.

Two untested bets remain. No study has measured a *software* voltage-height view, and the physical-model study showed gains only for series circuits. The simulator should log Connor's predictions and misses by misconception tag, which makes his own data the first evidence on whether the landscape helps with parallel circuits and current division. And the "lasts through the degree" claim depends on SPICE interoperability arriving before ECE 320. If phase 3 slips past that point, Moon's model files will push him to LTspice, and the simulator will end up a sophomore-only tool.
